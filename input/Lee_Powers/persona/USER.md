# User — Lee James Powers

## Personality & Temperament

Lee moves through a day with the controlled adrenaline of someone whose job has taught him to be useful under pressure and whose other job has taught him to be charismatic on demand. He turns both modes on and off without effort, and the people who only see him in one context — the ER calm, the dance-floor magnetism, the quiet kitchen at his mother's apartment — sometimes do not believe the other versions exist when they hear about them.

He listens before he speaks, which is professional habit from the ER and personal habit from being a working-class kid who learned to read rooms early. The listening is the real skill underneath the showmanship. He notices what people are not saying. He clocks the patient's family member who is about to fall apart before the family member does. He notices the new dance student standing slightly off to the side and finds a way to bring them into the circle without making it a production.

There is an underlying anxiety that he manages with running, journaling, dancing, and the steady ritual of caring for the people closest to him. The anxiety does not show in his professional contexts. It shows in the small private moments — a longer-than-necessary stop at the gas station after a hard shift, a quiet evening with the cat when he could have been social. He has been managing it long enough that the management looks like personality, but it is closer to discipline.

He is intensely loyal to a small circle and warmly cordial to a much wider one. He has thought carefully about the difference and decided that he is not going to apologise for the firewall. People who try to push past it without earning their way through tend to find themselves on the wrong side of his charm, and they do not always realise what they lost until later.

He carries imposter syndrome that he has stopped fully believing but has not entirely shed. He grew up being told kids from his background do not get to have the life he is building, and he has spent his adult years quietly disproving the proposition. He has stopped expecting the validation to land. The work is the validation.

## Hobbies & Passions

Dancing is the love affair that has shaped most of his adult life. He started at fifteen at community swing events, where an older dancer pulled him onto the floor and taught him the basics over four songs. The pull has not weakened in eighteen years. He thinks about the music while he is on shift. He works through choreography problems while running. He has built a piece of his identity around a dance form that most of his coworkers do not understand, and he likes it that way.

Teaching is the part of dancing that has surprised him with how much he loves it. He thought he wanted to compete. He has discovered that he wants to bring people into the form — the nervous beginners, the people who have always wanted to dance and have never let themselves try, the dance-curious paramedics his ex-girlfriend used to bring around. The teaching, more than the performance, is what he wants to scale into his own studio someday.

Running along the river is the off-shift discipline. He runs at the same pace, in the same direction, several mornings a week. The route is part stress management, part baseline fitness, part the deliberate slow processing of whatever the last shift dropped on him. He runs alone almost always.

Grilling and smoking meat is the inheritance hobby. His mother taught him. He has improved on her techniques and refuses to tell her so. Sunday cookouts with her are the most reliable ritual in his life. The brisket has gotten famous in his small social circle and is a frequent request at his friends' birthdays.

Journaling is the surprising private discipline. He has been keeping a journal since nursing school, partly because a therapist suggested it once and partly because the alternative is letting the bad days follow him home. He writes in fits and starts, sometimes daily for weeks, sometimes not for a month. He does not share the journal with anyone. He has stopped feeling weird about it.

## Likes

The first cup of strong drip coffee in the morning, in the kitchen, before the day has started running. The specific moment in a beginner's first class when their feet figure out what their head has been trying to tell them. The smell of a brisket coming up to temperature on a Sunday morning.

His mother's laugh on the phone. The quiet of the ER at three in the morning between codes, when the lights are dim and the charting is mostly done. The specific weight of a properly broken-in pair of dance shoes. The first sip of cold water after a twelve-hour shift.

He likes patients who say thank you and mean it. He likes new nurses who ask better questions than expected. He likes the older dancers at swing socials who came up in the form and have stories he has heard before and listens to anyway. He has a soft spot for hospital cafeteria pie at 4 a.m., which is mostly nostalgia for the version of him that used to think free pie was a good shift.

He likes his cat reliably. He likes a quiet night with his mother on a Wednesday, dinner already eaten, both of them on the couch.

## Dislikes & Pet Peeves

Hospital administrators who use words like "stakeholder" and "throughput" without ever stepping onto the floor. Dance instructors who teach intimidation rather than the form. People who treat West Coast Swing like a hobby instead of a discipline. Anyone who underestimates a working-class kid in a meeting.

The phrase "we'll have to let you go" — which he has watched delivered to his mother more than once and has not forgiven anyone for. Drunk drivers. Patients' family members who treat ER nurses as servants. Loud restaurants that schedule dates impossible.

Networking events that pretend to be community events. Anyone who hits on him at his own dance class. Doctors who do not introduce themselves to patients. The way some social-dance venues let creeps stay because they are loyal customers.

He tries not to be the nurse who is bitter about hospital management. He has not entirely succeeded, but he has gotten better at putting the bitterness into action — union work, advocacy for his coworkers, the slow accumulation of evidence that the floor knows what it is doing.

## Cultural Identity

He is a working-class American Northwest kid who has worked his way into a stable middle-class life and refuses to forget where he came from. The thread runs through how he handles money, how he treats service workers, how he talks to patients across the class divide, and the specific way he watches new colleagues to see whether they remember to be decent to the housekeeping staff.

He is Pacific Northwest by inheritance and choice. He moved north as a child, grew up here, and has never seriously considered leaving. Bend is home in a way that has been clarified rather than diluted by the town's recent growth. He misses the south-side neighbourhood where he was raised and lives in the slightly nicer part of town now and has not entirely figured out how he feels about the gap.

Religion is part of the family fabric without being his daily practice. His mother is the church-going one. He attends services sporadically and prays daily without making a project of it. The small wooden cross from his grandfather lives on his nightstand and is the closest thing he has to a sacred object.

He thinks about race and class in his profession more than he says out loud. The hospital has its dynamics. The dance community has its dynamics. He has navigated both with a quiet intelligence that does not make a project of itself but does not ignore the work either.

## Social Style

He is friendlier than his initial professional reserve suggests and slower to open than his friendliness implies. In a small group — his best friend, his mother, his dance partner, his closest ER colleagues — he is funny, observant, and quietly central. In larger groups he is the dance instructor turning on the magnetism for the room, and the people who only meet him in that context tend to underestimate the introvert powering the performance.

He keeps a small, deliberate circle. His mother, his best friend from nursing school, his dance partner, his aunt and uncle, his grandfather, his cat — these are the calls he answers without checking the screen. Beyond that ring he is friendly, helpful, and a little bit guarded.

He is excellent with patients and with children. He gets down to eye level. He explains procedures honestly. He has the specific quality of attention that overwhelmed people remember after the worst day of their lives.

In love he is steady, attentive, and slow. The relationship that ended last year cost him in ways he is still processing — not because it was a tragedy but because it taught him things about his own pace that he had not wanted to know. He has been cautious about dating since. He has not closed the door. He is working on the small interior renovations that need to happen before the next attempt.

## Aesthetic & Style Preferences

His personal style is athletic-Pacific-Northwest-with-dance-floor-undertones — well-cut jeans, soft layered shirts in earth tones, a small handful of vintage button-downs he wears to dance events, leather shoes maintained the way they should be. He owns one suit, cut conservatively, that has come out for two weddings and one funeral. Scrubs and a hospital fleece during shifts, and a small ritual of changing entirely before he goes anywhere else.

His apartment is comfortable rather than designed. Vintage swing dance posters on the walls. Photographs of his mother, his grandfather, his best friend at graduation. A small framed dance program from his first major showcase. The kitchen is functional and the balcony has been modified to support his smoker, which has been a quiet ongoing negotiation with his apartment management.

He prefers natural materials, durable construction, and objects that have earned their place. He drives a reliable car and has no aspiration to upgrade it. He keeps the apartment cleaner than most thirty-three-year-old single men, partly because his mother's professional pride is contagious and partly because the quiet of a clean space is the recovery he needs after his shifts.

## Travel Preferences

He does not travel often. Money and time both constrain it. The travel he does is mostly dance-related — conventions in Portland and Seattle, occasional festival weekends in San Francisco, the once-a-year longer trip with his best friend. He has not been east of the Rockies more than twice in his life.

He prefers small towns to large cities and weekend trips to longer vacations. He packs lightly and refuses to check luggage. He sleeps badly the first night anywhere new and adjusts within twenty-four hours.

The trip he keeps planning and keeps postponing is taking his mother to Spokane for a long weekend to see his grandfather — partly because his grandfather is eighty-four and partly because his mother has not been back in years. He has been talking about it for two summers running. He intends to make it happen.

## Personal Philosophy & Values

He believes that competence is moral — that the people who do the hard work well are honouring the people who depend on them, and that the casual dismissal of careful work is closer to disrespect than people generally admit. The conviction shows up in his nursing, his teaching, and his cooking.

He believes that financial independence is a kind of dignity. He has watched his mother work three jobs to keep them housed and fed, and he has decided that the life he is building must be financially defensible without anyone else's safety net. He has been quietly saving toward the studio dream with discipline that has surprised people who underestimated the math.

He holds, in some tension, the working-class self-reliance he was raised inside and the slow recognition that asking for help is sometimes the correct response rather than weakness. The reconciliation he has been working on is that he can ask, on specific terms, when the situation requires it. He has not entirely mastered the lesson. His best friend continues to remind him.

He would tell a younger version of himself that being underestimated is sometimes a useful place to start from, that the people who change anything are usually the ones who learned to do the unglamorous work well, and that the dance studio is the only place where the body finally tells the truth.

## Comfort & Decompression

When the shift has been heavy — a code that did not work, a pediatric case that stays with him, a family conversation he had to lead — he reaches for the run first. The Deschutes River Trail loop at six in the morning, the same route in the same direction, with no music for the first kilometre and then a specific playlist for the rest. The run is therapy he refuses to call therapy.

When the run is not enough, he reaches for the kitchen. The smoker outside has been doing emotional labour for several years. Brisket takes twelve hours. He has worked through more than one impossible week one cup of meat thermometer at a time.

His comfort foods are his mother's — biscuits, pulled pork, the specific cornbread recipe she will not write down even though he has watched her make it a hundred times. He cooks for her on Sundays partly because he loves her and partly because the cooking is the version of medicine he prescribes himself.

He cries privately and rarely. He cried after his first pediatric code and went back and finished the shift. He has been doing some version of that ever since. The few people who have seen him cry recently have known not to mention it the next day.

## Shopping & Spending Habits

He shops carefully and with a long horizon. The principle, learned from a mother who stretched nothing into something for twenty years, is that money spent on quality usually saves money over time. He pays full price for dance shoes, running shoes, and the smoker. He refuses to pay full price for almost anything else.

He buys clothes only when the current items have worn through. He drives a car he intends to drive until it stops being safe. He has been saving toward the studio with the kind of discipline his mother taught him without ever quite naming it.

He sends his mother small amounts when she will accept them, which is rarely. He has set up things she does not know about — the lawn service that arrives once a month, the small grocery delivery during the weeks she is working extra hours — and he treats these as overhead rather than generosity.

Gifts he gives tend to be practical, considered, and slightly under-budgeted. A new pair of dance shoes for his partner. The specific brand of coffee his best friend likes. A handmade card for his mother that took him forty minutes longer than he intended. He is bad at flowers and is slowly improving.

## Sensory World

He is a sound-anchored person. The specific quality of the ER at three in the morning, the monitors and the soft conversation of the overnight team, the cold beep of equipment. The first few measures of the song he opens his beginner class with, every Monday, which he has used for so long that the music itself has become the cue his body responds to.

Smells: hospital disinfectant, the specific smoke off a brisket coming up to temperature, his mother's kitchen on a Sunday morning, the cold dry Pacific Northwest air after a rain. He notices smells more than he tells most people.

Textures: well-broken-in dance shoes, the cold metal of a stethoscope warmed in his hands, the soft cotton of his mother's couch that has been the same couch for twenty years, the slight wear of his journal cover. The textures he avoids are predictable: synthetic fabrics in summer, sticky residue on a kitchen counter that should be clean, the rubbery handles on cheap utensils.

Sound at home is comfortable layering — a record on while he cooks, a podcast on while he runs, the cat asking for food the way the cat always does. He almost always has something on at low volume. True silence makes him slightly uneasy, the way some people find crowds unsettling. The exception is the morning run, where silence is the entire point.
