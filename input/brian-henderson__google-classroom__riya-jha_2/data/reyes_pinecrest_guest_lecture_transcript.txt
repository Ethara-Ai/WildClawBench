[00:00:14] Reyes: Thanks for having me. Today I want to focus on the one
assumption that I think is most often glossed over in intro biostatistics
classes, and that's the censoring assumption underlying the Kaplan-Meier
estimator. If you take nothing else away from this hour, take this: the KM
estimator requires NON-INFORMATIVE censoring. Not independent — non-informative.
Those words get used interchangeably in a lot of textbooks and they shouldn't.

[00:02:41] What does non-informative mean? It means the fact that a subject
was censored at some time t tells you nothing about when their event would
have occurred if you had kept following them. The censoring time and the
event time can be associated through covariates — that is allowed — what
they cannot do is be associated AFTER conditioning on the covariates you
have. So "independent of the event mechanism" is a sufficient but not
necessary version of the assumption. Non-informative is the precise term.

[00:07:30] Now, what does a censored subject actually contribute to the KM
estimator? This is the second thing I see students get wrong. People say
"oh, censored subjects drop out and contribute nothing." That is not
correct. Censored subjects are in the risk set up to and including their
censoring time. Every event that happens BEFORE they are censored has their
person-time in the denominator. So they contribute partial information —
they contribute through the risk set n_i — they just don't contribute event
information after they leave. The estimator is using their data, just only
through the time they were observed.

[00:14:05] Let me show you the formula again so this is concrete. Ŝ(t) is
the product over event times t_i of (1 minus d_i over n_i). The n_i — the
size of the risk set just before t_i — is where censored subjects show up.
A subject censored at t = 5 is counted in n_i for every event time before
5. After 5, they're gone. But they were there. That's the partial
contribution.

[00:21:18] Type I censoring, Type II censoring, random censoring — these
are different mechanisms by which censoring can arise. We won't focus on
them today because the KM estimator's validity depends on the
non-informative assumption, not on which mechanism produced the censoring.
A study with random dropout can be perfectly fine; a study with Type I
administrative censoring can be biased if dropout is informative. The
mechanism label is less important than the property.

[00:28:50] Questions from the floor are about how this plays out in

let me just hammer the two points: non-informative is the precise word,
and censored subjects contribute through the risk set — they are not
"thrown away."
