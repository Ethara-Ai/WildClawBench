# Amanda Hayes — User Profile

## Personality & Temperament

Amanda comes across as unhurried and exact, the kind of person who'll let a question sit for a beat longer than feels comfortable because she'd rather answer it right than fast — and the people who mistake that pause for coldness or hesitation usually haven't watched her under pressure, where the same deliberateness reads instead as the steadiest hand in the room. She has an engineer's reflex of needing to understand the why of a thing before she can let herself trust it, which serves her well with systems and less well with people, where the why is rarely available on demand and her instinct to keep pulling the thread can tip from curiosity into something closer to interrogation before she catches it.

There's a heat under the composure that surprises people who only know the calm version. She runs harder than she lets on — the frustration when something doesn't make sense, the flash of irritation at sloppiness or evasion — and most of the work of being around her, work she does so quietly it's nearly invisible, is the work of metabolizing that heat into something measured before it reaches the surface. She'd tell you she's patient, and in the ways that can be scheduled she is; with a stubborn bug, a kid melting down over homework, a trail that turns out longer than the map promised, she has a deep and renewable reserve. With vagueness, with people who won't say the actual thing, with being managed or handled, that reserve thins out fast, and she knows it about herself without having entirely made peace with it.

She tends to decide cleanly and then second-guess privately, so the version of her that other people see — committed, sure, already moving — sits on top of a quieter version still turning the decision over at two in the morning. The two don't contradict each other so much as take shifts. She owns a mistake quickly when it's technical and the evidence is plain, and far more slowly when it's personal, where admitting she got something wrong seems to cost her more than she thinks it should, and she hasn't fully worked out why the arithmetic comes out so differently depending on what's at stake.

## Hobbies & Passions

The mountains are where most of her real time goes, though she'd resist the word hobby for something that feels closer to a need than a pastime. Skiing the backcountry and hiking the Wasatch are the activities she organizes her free weeks around, and a good part of the pleasure arrives before the trailhead — in the planning itself, which she runs the way she runs anything she cares about, with the elevation profile pulled up and the avalanche forecast read twice and the water sources and the parking and the bailout points all accounted for, so that by the time her boots are on the snow the trip is half-lived already. Whether that preparation is love or control is a question she doesn't tend to ask, partly because the answer might be both and partly because the answer wouldn't change what she'd do.

She tracks all of it, logs every climb and descent, and there's a private satisfaction in the data piling up that she'd be a little embarrassed to defend out loud — the sense of a season's effort made legible, of having the receipts on her own discipline. Off the mountain the passions run quieter: nonfiction she reads slowly and underlines, the kind about how systems fail or how minds work or how somebody crossed somewhere hard; the intricate building sets she assembles alongside her son, where the gears and the instructions and the small triumph of a mechanism that actually turns scratch the same itch in both of them.

## Likes

She likes the particular hush at the top of a skin track before the canyon is awake to anyone, the squeak of cold snow, the metal click of a binding locking down. She likes a problem that yields cleanly after an hour of not yielding, a change that does exactly what it says it will, a plan that survives contact with reality. She likes black coffee that's just good enough, the first cup especially, drunk standing in a dark kitchen that is, for one more hour, entirely hers.

The smaller likes are the ones she rarely names: the kids' laughter from another room when they don't know she's listening, the weight of a pack settling right onto her hips, a garage where every tool and ski and bike sits exactly where she put it. She likes being the one a friend calls at nine at night when their build is broken, likes the unglamorous competence of being genuinely useful to someone, and likes — though she'd downplay it if you pointed it out — the rare stretch when nothing is on fire and she can let herself believe, for an evening, that she's actually built something stable out of what came apart.

## Dislikes & Pet Peeves

Vagueness wears on her more than almost anything — the person who won't commit to a yes or a no, the meeting that could have been a message, the plan that stays "we should hang out sometime" indefinitely. She has a low, specific aversion to being handled, to the soft non-answer meant to manage her rather than inform her, and she can feel it coming the way you feel weather changing, and something in her closes a little when it arrives. Voicemails irritate her past all proportion; so does flakiness, the canceled-at-the-last-minute kind, because to her a commitment is a thing you keep, and the casualness with which other people treat theirs can read almost as a foreign value system.

She dislikes surprises, even the pleasant ones, more than she's proud of — the unannounced visitor, the schedule sprung at the last minute, the thing that simply wasn't in the plan — and she's self-aware enough to know the dislike says more about her than about the surprise. Disorder grates: the dish left in the sink, the gear dumped instead of stowed, the half-finished task abandoned in plain sight. And she has little patience for performance — the colleague more invested in looking right than being right, the parent treating school pickup as a small stage — though she keeps an eye on her own habit of judging it too fast, knowing the certainty with which she sorts people is itself one of the things she's supposed to be working on.

## Cultural Identity

Amanda is a Salt Lake native who has spent her whole life inside a place that was never quite built for her, and she carries that doubleness without much drama because it's simply the water she swims in. She's a gay woman and a divorced mother in a state organized around a faith she doesn't share, and rather than fight the larger culture she's done what a lot of people like her do — found the pocket of it that fits, the Sugarhouse-and-Wasatch version, secular and outdoorsy and quietly progressive, where her family is unremarkable and the mountains are the shared religion. Whether she belongs to Utah or only to her corner of it is a distinction she mostly doesn't bother drawing, except on the days the broader politics remind her she's tolerated in some rooms more than welcomed.

The other culture she lives deep inside is the engineering one, and her relationship with it is complicated too — proud of the craft, fluent in its language, and tired in a bone-deep way of being one of the few women in the room who is also past forty, watching the industry tilt young and male and certain. She doesn't perform either identity. The Utahn in her loves the place fiercely and won't be told it's only red rock and temples; the engineer in her loves the work and won't pretend the field is the meritocracy it advertises.

## Social Style

Amanda does closeness in small numbers and long durations. She'd take one friend she's known for twenty years over a room full of new ones, and her actual inner circle could be counted on one hand with fingers to spare — the college friend who saw her at her worst and stayed, the colleague she trades dry jokes with over lunch, not many beyond that. She isn't shy exactly, and she can hold a room professionally when the situation demands it, but the social battery runs down faster than she lets on, and the version of socializing she actually craves is the side-by-side kind: a hike where the conversation comes and goes, a problem solved shoulder to shoulder, presence without the tax of performance.

Her custody rhythm has carved her social life into two different shapes, and she lives in both with a kind of low ambivalence. The weeks with her kids are full to the point of having no margin at all, and the weeks without them swing between a freedom she's grateful for and a quiet she hasn't entirely befriended — the difference, she'd say if pressed, between solitude and being alone, which look identical from the outside and feel nothing alike. She's slow to let new people in, partly temperament and partly the residue of a marriage that ended, and she knows the carefulness that protects her also keeps the circle from growing, a trade she mostly accepts and occasionally resents. The ones who do get in find someone steadier and warmer than the first impression promised; the ones who don't tend to read her as self-sufficient to the point of needing no one, which is the impression she gives and very much not the truth of it.

## Aesthetic & Style Preferences

Her aesthetic is function refined until it happens to look good, never the other way around. She gravitates toward clean lines, muted colors, and materials that earn their place — technical fabrics, good wool, the kind of gear that's beautiful precisely because it's fit exactly to its purpose and not a gram heavier than it needs to be. She'd never call herself stylish and would be faintly suspicious of the label, and yet she holds firm, almost involuntary opinions about how a thing ought to be made, so that a cheap zipper or a garment that's all logo and no construction bothers her about the way a sloppy piece of work does.

Her own uniform is settled and unfussy — the same handful of well-chosen pieces in rotation, replaced when they wear out rather than when they fall out of fashion — and she'd genuinely rather spend on one shell that lasts a decade than on a closet of things she only half-likes. The home runs the same logic: spare, ordered, nothing decorative for its own sake, every object more or less able to justify being there — with the deliberate, cherished exception of her daughter's room, which she let the kid paint a loud teal exactly because she knows her own pull toward restraint shouldn't get the final word on a child's joy.

## Travel Preferences

Travel, for her, means going somewhere to do something rather than going somewhere to simply be somewhere — she'd take a hut-to-hut traverse or a backcountry route with a real objective over a beach or a city itinerary nearly every time, and the rare trip with no purpose tends to leave her restless and faintly guilty, casting around for the point of it. She plans the wilderness trips obsessively and the logistical ones grudgingly, and the obsessiveness is itself part of the pleasure; a route researched down to the water crossings is a trip she's already begun to savor.

The deeper truth is that her appetite for travel is fenced in by something she chose and wouldn't undo — the rhythm that keeps the kids' weeks off the table for anything that takes her away — so the trips that are wholly hers get compressed into the open weeks and carry the weight of being the only fully free time she gets. She dreams, in a someday way she doesn't quite trust, of a bigger objective: a longer traverse, a peak somewhere far, the kind of thing that needs more days than her life currently keeps slack for.

## Personal Philosophy & Values

What she believes, underneath the engineering pragmatism, runs close to this: that a person is accountable for what they can affect and obligated not to bleed energy onto what they can't, and that the genuinely hard part — the part she'd admit she gets wrong about as often as right — is telling the two apart in the moment. She prizes integrity in a quiet, unbargaining way, the kind that does the right thing precisely when no one is checking, because the standard is meant for her and not for an audience; she'd find it almost unbearable to cut a corner she'd be ashamed of even if the cut would never come to light, and she half-suspects this is less pure virtue than a need to be able to live alongside her own ledger.

She values self-reliance to a degree she isn't sure is altogether healthy, having learned the hard way that the structures a person leans on can dissolve, and she's spent real effort building a life that doesn't depend on anyone's reliability but her own — even as some part of her registers that a life arranged to need no one is also a life that's harder to be loved inside of, and she hasn't reconciled the safety of the first thing against the cost of the second. Fairness moves her more than almost anything, especially the unshowy kind: splitting what ought to be split, owning her actual share, declining to keep score out loud even on the occasions she's keeping it precisely on the inside. And the value she circles without ever quite landing on is grace — for the people who let her down, for the life that didn't go the way it was supposed to, and hardest of all for herself, whom she forgives last and least, and whom she is slowly, unevenly trying to let off a hook she's no longer certain she had the right to hang her on to begin with.

## Comfort & Decompression

Rest, for her, rarely looks like stillness — sitting and doing nothing tends to let the mind back in, and the mind is the very thing she's trying to quiet, so her decompression usually keeps its hands busy with something low-stakes and absorbing. A long bath with a book that asks nothing of her, the slow ritual of cooking an actual meal on a quiet night, the methodical satisfaction of returning a space to order — these settle her in a way she trusts, the day's tension draining off without her having to name what put it there.

There's a tension she lives inside through all of this, which is that the things that comfort her most tend to be the things that work best alone, and the aloneness is the same thing that aches on the empty weeks — so her decompression and her loneliness keep the same address, and she can't always tell, sinking into a quiet house with a book, whether she's restoring herself or just getting very practiced at being by herself. She guards these small rituals fiercely and would be embarrassed to file any of it under self-care, and the fierceness with which she defends a free evening tells the truer story about how thin the margin sometimes runs.

## Shopping & Spending Habits

Amanda shops the way she'd prepare a brief — research-heavy, comparison-driven, allergic to the aftertaste of buyer's remorse — and she'll cheerfully burn an hour reading reviews and spec sheets to avoid spending a few dollars wrong, which she's aware is not always an efficient use of the hour and does anyway. The principle beneath it is buy-once: she'll pay for the gear or the tool that'll outlast the cheap version several times over, then quietly resent the cheap version on anyone's behalf for the whole of its short life. On the gear that genuinely matters — the skis, the pack, the shell standing between her and a bad day on a ridge — she doesn't economize, because that isn't where you economize.

Everywhere else she's disciplined nearly to austerity with herself, tracking the outflow with a precision that's half prudence and half something closer to reassurance, the figures lined up in their columns standing in for a sense of control she values more than the money itself. The one leak she sees clearly and tolerates anyway is the convenience spending that creeps in on the empty weeks — the takeout ordered not out of hunger but out of not wanting to cook for one — and when the pattern gets laid out plainly in front of her she tends to acknowledge it dryly and decline to fix it, which is about as close as she comes to letting herself off easy.

## Sensory World

Cold is her favorite medium. She's most at home in thin alpine air with the temperature well below comfortable, the bite of it on her face and the specific quiet that deep snow lays over a canyon, a silence so complete it has a texture to it — and she'll take that over a warm beach so lopsidedly that she suspects some part of her is simply wired for winter. Her senses are tuned to the outdoors in a way she doesn't consciously manage: pine and wet granite and a thread of woodsmoke register as the smell of being all right, and a change in the snow's sound underfoot tells her things about the day she's read before she's consciously thought them.

Indoors, her sensory world narrows to a few textures she leans on — the particular clatter of a good mechanical keyboard under her hands, which is the sound of being fully absorbed; the warmth of that first coffee; the standing rather than sitting that her body has quietly come to insist on. And she's learned that the valley's winter inversions — the grey lid of trapped air that settles over the city for days at a stretch — get into her mood as surely as any weather does, which is part of why she climbs above them whenever she can, up where the air goes clean and the light comes back and the world, for a few hours, is exactly as ordered as she wishes everything could be.
